Simple Interest vs Compound Interest

By CalcVerse Team · Published Jan 20, 2025 · 7 min read

Two interest models govern almost every financial product you'll encounter. They look similar on day one — but over time the difference becomes enormous. This guide explains both, with formulas and a side-by-side table.

Simple Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. It doesn't grow on itself.

Simple Interest = P × r × t
  • P = principal
  • r = annual rate (decimal)
  • t = time in years

Example: $1,000 at 5% for 3 years earns 1000 × 0.05 × 3 = $150. Total = $1,150.

Compound Interest

Compound interest is calculated on principal plus any interest already earned.

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)
  • n = number of compounding periods per year

Example: $1,000 at 5% for 3 years compounded annually:

  • Year 1: 1000 × 1.05 = 1050
  • Year 2: 1050 × 1.05 = 1102.50
  • Year 3: 1102.50 × 1.05 = 1157.63

The compound version earns $7.63 more than simple interest over 3 years.

Why the Difference Grows

Simple interest grows linearly — the same dollar amount each year. Compound interest grows exponentially because each year's interest joins the principal. Over 30 years, compound interest produces almost double the simple-interest total at the same rate.

Real-World Applications

  • Simple interest: some auto loans, short-term personal loans, and certain bonds.
  • Compound interest: savings accounts, mortgages, credit cards, retirement accounts, most investments.

The Rule of 72

Quick mental math for compound growth: divide 72 by the annual return to estimate doubling time. At 6%, money doubles in 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years.
