What Is Compound Interest?

Compound interest is interest earned on both your original principal and on interest that has already accumulated. It's the reason a modest savings account can grow substantially over decades — and also why credit card debt spirals quickly.

How to Use This Calculator

  1. Enter your initial principal.
  2. Enter an annual interest rate (as a percentage).
  3. Enter the number of years.
  4. Choose the compounding frequency.
  5. (Optional) Add a monthly contribution to model regular deposits.
  6. Click Calculate.

Compound Interest Formula

For a lump sum with no additional contributions:

A = P × (1 + r/n)^(n×t)
  • A = final amount
  • P = principal
  • r = annual rate (decimal)
  • n = compounding periods per year
  • t = years

For monthly contributions (future value of an annuity):

FV_contrib = PMT × [ (1 + i)^m − 1 ] ÷ i

Where i is the monthly rate and m is the number of months.

How the Calculation Works

For each compounding period, interest is added to the balance, and the next period's interest is computed on the larger balance. More frequent compounding means slightly higher effective returns — the difference between monthly and daily compounding is small, but over decades it adds up.

Example Calculation

$10,000 at 7% for 20 years, compounded monthly:

  • A = 10000 × (1 + 0.07/12)^(12×20)
  • A = 10000 × (1.005833)^240
  • A ≈ $40,387 — a 4× increase from interest alone.

Understanding Your Result

The result is the future value. The detail box shows how much of that came from your own contributions versus how much was earned in interest. The table shows growth year by year so you can see compounding accelerate.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing APR with APY — APY already accounts for compounding.
  • Forgetting that inflation reduces real returns. A 7% return with 3% inflation is only ~4% real growth.
  • Assuming returns are guaranteed. Investments can lose value.
  • Ignoring taxes on interest earnings (especially in taxable accounts).
